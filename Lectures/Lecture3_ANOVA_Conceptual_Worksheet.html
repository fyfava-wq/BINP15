Exit ticket

ANOVA is useful when I want to understand… Is there a difference between group means.

1. What ANOVA is for

When predictor variables are categorical (factors), linear models can be used to perform analyses of variance (ANOVA). Parameter estimation works much like regression, except that the model estimates group effects and contrasts between factor levels rather than regression slopes.

The aim of ANOVA is to partition variation in a response into additive components. In a one-way ANOVA, total variation is divided into variation among groups and variation within groups (the residual variation):

\[ SS_T = SS_{among} + SS_{within} \]

The total sum of squares uses each observation’s deviation from the grand mean. The among-groups sum of squares describes variation associated with group means. The within-groups or residual sum of squares uses each observation’s deviation from its own group mean. ANOVA evaluates whether the among-groups variation is large relative to the within-groups variation expected by chance.

A statistically supported omnibus test indicates that at least one group mean differs from the others. It does not, by itself, identify which groups differ.

2. Assumptions and visualizing groups

For the ANOVA model described in the course, residuals are assumed to be normally distributed within each group and variances are assumed to be equal across groups. Boxplots can help inspect the distributions across factor levels. By default, the box spans the first to third quartiles, the thick line marks the median, whiskers extend to 1.5 times the interquartile range, and individual points beyond the whiskers are shown as outliers.

Boxplots summarize the data; plots that show individual observations can make the pattern more visible. A jittered scatterplot can be combined with a boxplot to reduce overlap and show the observations themselves.

3. One-way ANOVA example

The chapter simulates body-size measurements for three groups. The code is reproducible because it sets the random seed.

set.seed(100)

groups <- as.factor(rep(c("Low", "Medium", "High"), each = 50))
x <- c(
  rnorm(50, 10, 3),
  rnorm(50, 13, 3),
  rnorm(50, 14, 3)
)

# Inspect the response across factor levels
boxplot(x ~ groups,
        las = 1,
        xlab = "",
        ylab = "Body size (g)")

Fit a linear model with lm(), then pass the fitted model to anova():

m <- lm(x ~ groups)
anova(m)
## Analysis of Variance Table
## 
## Response: x
##            Df  Sum Sq Mean Sq F value    Pr(>F)    
## groups      2  319.97 159.985  19.591 2.866e-08 ***
## Residuals 147 1200.43   8.166                      
## ---
## Signif. codes:  0 '***' 0.001 '**' 0.01 '*' 0.05 '.' 0.1 ' ' 1

Reading the ANOVA table

  • Degrees of freedom (Df): for a factor with k levels, the factor has k - 1 degrees of freedom. Residual degrees of freedom are n - k, where n is the number of observations.
  • Sum Sq: sums of squares quantify variation. Adding the factor and residual sums of squares gives the total sum of squares.
  • Mean Sq: each sum of squares divided by its degrees of freedom.
  • F value: factor mean square divided by residual mean square. This compares among-group variation with within-group variation.
  • Pr(>F): the p-value for the omnibus test of whether the group means are all equal, under the model assumptions.

For the simulated data there are 3 groups and 150 observations, so factor df is 3 - 1 = 2 and residual df is 150 - 3 = 147.

The total sum of squares divided by n - 1 is the sample variance. The following code calculates the total sum of squares from the ANOVA components and compares it with var(x):

a <- anova(m)
SS_T <- sum(a$`Sum Sq`)

SS_T
## [1] 1520.4
SS_T / (length(x) - 1)
## [1] 10.20403
var(x)
## [1] 10.20403

The proportion of total variation explained by the group factor is the model’s R-squared:

SS_among <- a["groups", "Sum Sq"]
R2_from_SS <- SS_among / SS_T
R2_from_SS
## [1] 0.2104517

For this one-factor model, the same quantity can be written as:

\[ R^2 = \frac{SS_{among}}{SS_T} = 1 - \frac{SSE}{SST} \]

where SSE is the residual (error) sum of squares and SST is the total sum of squares.

With R’s default treatment contrasts, the intercept is the estimated mean of the reference factor level. The other factor coefficients are contrasts from that reference level. The associated t-tests test whether each group’s mean differs from the reference group’s mean.

In this example, the factor levels are ordered alphabetically, so High is the reference level. To choose another reference group, set the factor levels explicitly:

groups <- factor(groups, levels = c("Low", "Medium", "High"))
m_low_reference <- lm(x ~ groups)
summary(m_low_reference)

The parameter estimates describe the magnitude and direction of the group differences. A contrast can also be expressed relative to the reference mean. For example, the chapter’s Low-versus-High contrast is approximately -3.456 / 13.701 = -0.252, or 25.2% lower relative to the High-group mean.

The omnibus ANOVA test and coefficient tests answer related but different questions: the ANOVA test asks whether any group means differ, while each treatment-contrast test compares one level with the reference level.

5. Estimating a mean for each group

To estimate a mean for each factor level directly, fit a model without an intercept by adding -1 to the formula. confint() then returns confidence intervals for the estimated means.

m_means <- lm(x ~ groups - 1)
summary(m_means)$coef

6. Key takeaways

  1. ANOVA is a linear-model approach for categorical predictors.
  2. It partitions total variation into among-groups and within-groups (residual) components.
  3. The F statistic compares factor mean square with residual mean square.
  4. A supported omnibus test means that at least one group mean differs; it does not name the differing groups.
  5. In a model with treatment contrasts, the intercept is the reference-group mean and factor coefficients are contrasts from that level.
  6. Choose the reference factor level deliberately and report effect sizes in interpretable units.
